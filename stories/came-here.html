Stories from these waters · People and arrivals · Locust Point, 1868–1914

Came here.

Between 1868 and 1914, ships from Bremen tied up at a railroad pier on Locust Point, and more than a million people, by the museum's count, walked off them into a new country. Most boarded trains for the Midwest the same day. The ones who stayed built neighborhoods that still stand around the harbor.

Before the pier

Baltimore's ties to Bremen, the German port, went back to the 1790s, when its merchants traded tobacco across the Atlantic. By the 1830s, ships that carried tobacco east carried emigrants west, and Fells Point was where most of them landed. Before the Civil War, by one historian's count, about four in five immigrants arriving in Baltimore were German. The Irish came next, many of them to dig the turnpikes and canals and lay track for the Baltimore and Ohio Railroad, and many more during the famine years of the 1840s and 1850s. By 1860, about a quarter of the city was foreign-born.

They did not arrive to a welcome. From the mid-1850s to 1860, Baltimore was governed by the American Party, known as the Know-Nothings, whose platform was hostility to immigrants and Catholics. Its street gangs, with names like the Plug Uglies and the Blood Tubs, beat voters who looked German or Irish, kidnapped men and forced them to vote again and again, and turned election days into battles. Estimates of the dead in the 1856 elections alone run from 17 to 30. Maryland was the only state that voted for the party's presidential candidate, Millard Fillmore, that year.

The same decade was violent on the docks. White mobs drove Black caulkers out of shipyards in Fells Point, and some owners stopped hiring them. The people who worked this waterfront were competing for the same jobs, and the lines between them were drawn in more than one direction.

The deal

In early 1867, John W. Garrett, president of the B&O, and Albert Schumacher, the Baltimore agent of the North German Lloyd steamship line, struck an agreement. The railroad would build an immigration pier at Locust Point and connect it to its tracks. The Lloyd would send ships from Bremen. Immigrants could buy a single ticket that covered the crossing and the train west at a discount.

On March 23, 1868, the Lloyd's steamship Baltimore docked at Locust Point after twenty days at sea from Bremen. The city turned out with a parade. The immigration station came to stand on B&O Pier 9, on the harbor side of the peninsula, near the foot of today's Hull Street.

The design was simple. Passengers walked off the ship and onto waiting trains. Photographs from 1904 show families from the steamship Neckar on the pier with their bundles, sorted into pens by destination. By the 1880s the railroad had built an inspection station on the pier, and federal inspection was formalized in the 1890s.

Who came

Through the 1880s most arrivals were still German, among them Catholics leaving Bavaria in the 1870s. Czech families from Bohemia and the first Poles followed. Jews from Central and Eastern Europe came through the Locust Point docks as well; Baltimore's Jewish population grew from about 10,000 in 1880 to about 24,000 in 1890. After about 1890 the ships carried more people from the Russian and Austro-Hungarian empires: Poles, Lithuanians, Czechs, Slovaks, Ukrainians, Hungarians and Russian Jews.

Most did not stay. The B&O's lines ran to Cincinnati, Chicago and St. Louis, and in the first years perhaps two-thirds or more of the passengers rode on to the Midwest. Those who stayed settled close to the work: Poles in Fells Point and Canton, Czechs in a corner of East Baltimore they called Little Bohemia, Lithuanians in West Baltimore, Russian Jews in East Baltimore, the Irish near the B&O shops at Mount Clare.

The work

The water and the railroad hired them. Irish families built and ran the B&O; one of their homes on Lemmon Street is now the Irish Railroad Workers Museum. Locust Point's rowhouses filled with dockworkers and men from the factories that grew up around the B&O terminals.

Baltimore became the country's leading producer of canned food, built on Chesapeake oysters and Maryland farms. Polish families, along with Czechs, Germans, Italians and African Americans, shucked oysters and worked the canneries side by side. Many followed a yearly circuit: canneries in Baltimore in summer, berry and tomato fields on Maryland farms, then oyster canneries on the Gulf Coast in winter. The photographer Lewis Hine documented their children at work in those fields and sheds.

In East Baltimore, hundreds of small garment shops employed Russian Jewish, Bohemian, Italian and Lithuanian workers, about half of them women and girls.

Newcomers who needed a bed could find one five blocks from the pier. In 1904, a German congregation, Christ Church, opened the Immigrant House at 1308 Beason Street, offering room and board, help finding work, and English lessons. By its pastor's count, it had taken in 3,710 guests by 1916.

The end of the pier

The First World War ended it. When war broke out in 1914, the North German Lloyd's service stopped. On October 30, 1917, the B&O piers at Locust Point caught fire, and the old immigration building on Pier 9 burned. Four men died. Germans and Austrians were arrested on suspicion of sabotage, but investigators later traced the fire to electrical wiring.

Why this story, now

Families across the country can trace an ancestor to a ship that docked here. Many came through this one pier, then scattered west by rail.

The newcomers met the people already working this water. In the decades after 1864, newly free Black Marylanders and newly landed Europeans worked the same docks, the same oyster boats and the same canneries. Sometimes they competed for those jobs, sometimes violently. Often they did the same hard work at the same tables. Both are true, and the second is easy to forget.

The pier is gone, but its story is kept close by. The Baltimore Immigration Museum, in the old Immigrant House at 1308 Beason Street, opened in 2016. Liberty Garden, a memorial to the arrivals, stands near the old pier site at 900 Hull Street. Fort McHenry, which the ships passed on their way in, still watches the channel.

Where the accounts differ

We checked this story against federal records, newspapers, scholarship and public-history sources. Where they disagree, here is what we found and what we wrote.

  • How busy the port was. Baltimore is often called the second-busiest immigrant port after New York. The museum's own historian and the federal arrival tables we checked put it usually third, behind New York and Boston, and second in some years. We avoid the ranking.
  • How many arrived. The figure of about 1.2 million for Locust Point comes from the Baltimore Immigration Memorial and its historian. Other accounts give about 1 million for the pier, or 1.5 million to nearly 2 million for the whole port over longer periods. We give the museum's figure and say whose it is.
  • The yearly peak. A peak of about 40,000 a year is often repeated. Federal reports show about 56,000 in each of 1903 and 1904. We give no peak.
  • When the deal was made. Accounts give January 1867, late 1866 or early 1867, or 1868, the year the pier opened. We say early 1867.
  • Which pier. Accounts name Pier 8, Piers 8 and 9, or Piers 6 through 9. The 1904 photographs and the 1917 fire reports place the immigration building on Pier 9.
  • When inspection began. Sources give an inspection station leased to the federal government in 1887, federal inspection of every immigrant from 1891, and inspection on board as ships entered the Bay. We say the station was built in the 1880s and federal inspection formalized in the 1890s.
  • The 1917 fire. It is often called mysterious. Reports at the time suspected sabotage; later findings blamed wiring. We give both.
  • Know-Nothing violence. Counts of the dead in 1856 range from 17 to 30. We give the range.
  • Who came through the pier. Italians and Greeks are often listed among Locust Point's arrivals, but most came to Baltimore by train from New York or Philadelphia. We leave them out of the pier's story and include them in the city's work.
  • How many stayed. One study of early passenger lists found about a quarter stayed in Baltimore, falling to about a tenth by the 1890s; another history says perhaps two-thirds of the first arrivals went west. We say most went on.
