The people of the rivers

When the English arrived in 1607, tens of thousands of people lived around the Chesapeake. Estimates run from more than 30,000, by the historian James D. Rice, to about 50,000, by the National Park Service.⁠1,3 They belonged to many nations. On the Virginia side, about 30 chiefdoms paid tribute to the paramount chief Powhatan.⁠1,4 On the north bank of the Potomac, the Piscataway answered to a paramount chief, the tayac, whose seat was at Moyaone.⁠11,27

Across the Bay, the Nanticoke held much of the Eastern Shore. Their name for themselves is given as People of the Tidewater.⁠30,27 The Choptank, the Pocomoke, the Assateague and others lived nearby.⁠29,16 At the head of the Bay, the Susquehannock, who spoke an Iroquoian language, lived in palisaded towns along the Susquehanna.⁠13

Rivers as roads

The rivers carried travel, trade and war. Dugout canoes moved people, fish and goods between towns.⁠1,2 Fishermen built weirs, fences of stakes that ran out from shore for as much as 1,000 feet and steered fish into traps. Boys dove for oysters. When the oysters on a bed grew small, people moved to another bed.⁠2

The shell mounds along the Bay hold the record. They show oyster harvests going back as far as 3,200 years, and the shells stay large across those thousands of years. Archaeologists read that as a harvest the reefs could sustain, though the lead researcher cautions it may not have been a deliberate plan.⁠6

Many names on today's map come from these nations: Patapsco, Patuxent, Potomac, Choptank, Nanticoke, Pocomoke. Chesapeake itself comes from an Algonquian word that English colonists first wrote down in the 1580s. Its meaning is less settled than the signs suggest. "Great shellfish bay" is the popular reading. The Chesapeake Bay Program says historians are uncertain, and the linguist Blair Rudes said it may instead have meant "great water" or named a village at the Bay's mouth.⁠7

Up the Patapsco, 1608

On June 12, 1608, John Smith's crew rowed into the Patapsco, the river that becomes Baltimore's harbor. They saw no one on shore.⁠5 Historians think this stretch was a borderland between the Algonquian peoples to the south and the Susquehannock and Massawomeck to the north. No Native town at the Inner Harbor appears in the record.

Smith's map of the Bay, published in 1612, marks dozens of towns elsewhere on its shores. Much of what it shows came from Native guides.⁠4

The colony

In 1634 the Ark and the Dove brought Maryland's first colonists up the Potomac. Leonard Calvert asked the Piscataway tayac for permission to settle. The tayac answered that "he would not bid him goe, neither would hee bid him stay, but that he might use his owne discretion."⁠31,8 The colonists settled at a Yaocomaco village that became St. Mary's City. The Jesuit Andrew White wrote that they bought "thirtie miles of ground" for "axes, hoes, cloth and hatchets."⁠9 The English counted it a purchase. The Yaocomaco did not share that idea of owning land.⁠10 Some left at once. The rest stayed through the harvest.⁠10,31

Over the next century, disease, war and treaties took most of the land. A 1666 treaty with the Piscataway recognized their rights to hunt, fish and crab, and set aside reservations. Later treaties were broken.⁠11,27 In 1669 the colony set aside land for the Choptank at Locust Neck. A state highway marker unveiled there in 2024 tells its story.⁠15 In 1675, Maryland and Virginia militia besieged a Susquehannock fort on Piscataway Creek and killed five leaders who came out to talk. The violence that followed helped set off Bacon's Rebellion in Virginia.⁠12

Many moved north. The Piscataway left southern Maryland for Virginia in 1697, and in 1699 settled on Heater's Island in the Potomac, where smallpox killed 57 people in 1704. By 1718 they were in Pennsylvania.⁠14 Nanticoke families went north from the 1740s, and some settled with the Six Nations in what is now Ontario.⁠27 Susquehannock descendants live today among the Seneca-Cayuga and the Six Nations.⁠27,13

Others stayed. Piscataway families kept to the land around Port Tobacco, farming and holding to St. Ignatius Church. The church recorded them as Indian. Census takers often recorded them as "mulatto" or "Negro."⁠11

Still here

Maryland recognized the Piscataway Indian Nation and the Piscataway Conoy Tribe on January 9, 2012, the state's first recognized tribes. It recognized the Accohannock Indian Tribe on December 19, 2017.⁠16,17,18 The orders grant no land or gaming rights, and they came after earlier requests had been denied.⁠11,17 No federally recognized tribe is based in Maryland. The Nause-Waiwash Band of Indians, the Assateague Peoples Tribe and the Pocomoke Indian Nation are among the communities that work with the state's Commission on Indian Affairs.⁠16

In Delaware, the Nanticoke hold land near Millsboro, run a museum and host a powwow every year.⁠19 Virginia has seven federally recognized tribes. Congress recognized six of them in 2018.⁠28

The Reservation on Baltimore Street

Baltimore's largest Native community came by road. After the Second World War, Lumbee families left Robeson County, North Carolina, for work in Baltimore's factories and building trades.⁠23,20,21 They settled in East Baltimore between Upper Fells Point and Washington Hill, a few blocks from the harbor. The heart of it was the 1700 block of East Baltimore Street, and the community called it the Reservation.⁠20,21

At its height the community numbered in the thousands. Estimates run from 2,000 to 7,000.⁠21,22 Urban renewal leveled most of that block in the early 1970s. By then, pushed by renewal and other pressures, many families had begun moving out to Dundalk, Essex and Middle River.⁠20,21,22

In 1968, community members founded what became the Baltimore American Indian Center. It has stood at 113 South Broadway since 1972. In September 2025 it was added to the National Register of Historic Places.⁠23 On December 18, 2025, 69 years after a 1956 law recognized them in name only, the Lumbee received full federal recognition.⁠24

Why this story, now

The first two parts of this series asked how people came to these waters. This part begins with the people who were already here.

The Bay's future is still being decided, and tribes are asking for a seat. In December 2025, at the National Aquarium in the Inner Harbor, the Chesapeake Executive Council directed its staff to work with federally recognized tribes on joining the Bay Program partnership. In June 2026 the decision was postponed. A resolution is due for consideration in December 2026.⁠25 Maryland has no federally recognized tribe, so Maryland's nations are not part of that process.⁠16,25

In Virginia, the Nansemond Indian Nation plants oysters in a creek its people have fished for centuries.⁠26

These waters were theirs first. Many of their nations are still here.

Where the accounts differ

We checked this story against archival, scholarly, tribal, state and public-history sources. Where they disagree, here is what we found and what we wrote.

  • How many people lived around the Bay. Estimates for 1607 run from more than 30,000 (James D. Rice) to about 50,000 (National Park Service). For Powhatan's chiefdom alone, figures run from about 8,000 to more than 22,000. We give the range for the Bay.
  • What "Chesapeake" means. "Great shellfish bay" is widely repeated. The Chesapeake Bay Program calls the meaning uncertain, and the linguist Blair Rudes proposed "great water" or a village at the Bay's mouth. We give the popular reading and say it is disputed. The meanings of Susquehanna, Choptank and Pocomoke are disputed too, so we give none.
  • The 1634 land. Some accounts say the colonists received about 30 acres. Andrew White's own account says "thirtie miles of ground." We quote White and leave the measure as he wrote it.
  • Who the Piscataway leader was in 1634. Sources disagree on whether Wannas was the Piscataway tayac or the Yaocomaco leader, so we do not name him.
  • Which tribes Maryland recognizes. Some accounts say the Pocomoke were recognized with the Accohannock in 2017. The Maryland Manual lists three recognized tribes, and the Pocomoke Indian Nation among the groups that work with the state. We follow the Manual.
  • The Susquehannock. Some references call them extinct as a tribe. Britannica and others record living descendants. We say they live today among other nations.
  • The Lumbee community's size. Counts at its height run from 2,000 to 7,000, and censuses of the time often classed Lumbee residents as white. We give the range.
  • The Bay Program decision. Recommendations on tribal partnership were due July 1, 2026, and the decision was postponed to December 2026. It covers federally recognized tribes only. We will update this story when it is made.
  • When the Yaocomaco left St. Mary's. A 1635 account says the rest agreed to leave at the end of harvest. A later account says they stayed a year. We say they stayed through the harvest.
  • The Nanticoke name. It is often given as "Tidewater People." We use "People of the Tidewater," the wording of the tribe's own museum.
